When machines became capable of beating any grandmaster at chess, chess became more of a sport than a measure of human intelligence. Similarly, now that AI and machines have become capable of deep thinking, logical reasoning, and solving some of the toughest problems, what is going to be the benchmark for human intelligence?

It turns out that deep logical reasoning is mechanical, too. Does this mean that human intuition is something gained through repeated exposure, or is it something more uniquely human? What about creativity? Can machines be genuinely creative?

Genuine creativity is something I suspect machines cannot replicate. But this begs us to ask a deeper question: where does human creativity come from?

Is it a product of human lived experience? If so, I would argue that machines cannot replicate it in the same way humans do.

For a simple reason: machines cannot live human lives or experience human experiences. Surprisingly, I am not saying that machines cannot have lived experiences of their own. Perhaps they could. There is currently no conclusive way to determine whether they do or do not. However, we can say with a high degree of confidence that their experiences, if any, would be different from ours.

I am speaking about *subjective experience* here. I will not delve too deeply into the concept, as I am neither a philosopher nor a psychologist qualified to comment on it in depth. For the purposes of this argument, we do not need to define subjective experience precisely.

In philosophical discussions, as Bertrand Russell and other philosophers have explored in different ways, there is a fundamental difficulty in knowing whether anyone other than oneself has subjective experience. But for now, let us take a common-sense approach. I claim with absolute certainty that I have subjective experience. Obviously, I do not need to prove it to myself. The experience itself is the basis of my claim.

Now, I claim with a very high degree of confidence that my friend has subjective experience. If you are a philosophy student or enthusiast, you would know why I cannot claim the same degree of certainty in both cases. Similarly, I can reasonably extend this belief to other human beings.

There is a catch, though. This line of reasoning is inferential. I arrive at the conclusion because a fellow human being reacts to pain and pleasure in ways similar to how I do. Since I know that I experience pain and pleasure, I have good reason to believe that other human beings experience them too.

I can extend the same argument to my cat and other mammals. However, I become less certain about the nature of their experiences because their responses and ways of interacting with the world differ from ours. This uncertainty increases as we consider organisms that are more different from us. For instance, we cannot confidently say that plants experience pain in the same subjective sense that humans do, despite being biological entities like us. Their responses to stimuli are very different from ours.

So, through common-sense reasoning alone, it is difficult to determine whether intelligent machines have subjective experience.

I am open to changing my views and listening to arguments. If someone argues that AI systems already have subjective experience or will eventually develop it, I would not dismiss the possibility outright. It is a possibility. Even though many discussions on this subject are vague, and people often make arguments without clearly defining what they mean, I am willing to give them the benefit of the doubt for the time being.

But here is the most important argument: even if machines do have subjective experience, in whatever sense, it could be very, very different from human subjective experience. This does not require much explanation. It seems reasonable to assume that their experiences, if any, are different from ours unless there is sufficient evidence to suggest otherwise. The burden of proof lies with those who claim that they are the same or similar.

And I say that as long as they are different, machines cannot be creative in the same way humans are. They can, of course, mimic creativity, as humans do too, but not necessarily be genuinely creative in the sense I am describing. Genuine creativity, I believe, requires inspiration: a magical moment in an individual's life that emerges from their subjective experience, their own lived experience.

A machine may try to mimic this, but the result may lack the same meaning for the machine itself. This is difficult to prove through logical argument because we are discussing something rooted in human experience. Most human beings may intuitively understand what I am trying to say, but it is difficult to explain through purely logical reasoning.

So, I will resort to what our ancient ancestors often did when trying to convey difficult ideas: tell a story.

## Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote

Jorge Luis Borges tells a strange story about a fictional writer named **Pierre Menard**. Menard sets himself an almost absurd task: to write *Don Quixote* again, independently producing the same passages word for word. Somehow, he succeeds.

The text he produces is identical to Cervantes's.

And yet Borges suggests that the two works are not really the same.

Cervantes wrote those words in seventeenth-century Spain, shaped by his own life, his experiences, and the world he inhabited. Menard wrote the very same words centuries later, from an entirely different life and perspective. **The sentences are identical, but what stands behind them is not.**

Perhaps this is the distinction we need to consider when we talk about machine creativity.

A machine may produce something that looks creative. It may produce a painting, a story, a melody, or an idea that is indistinguishable from something produced by a human. But is producing something that *looks* creative the same as actually being creative?

I do not think we can answer that question simply by looking at the result.

A perfect imitation can reproduce the appearance of the original so completely that we cannot tell them apart. But **resemblance is not identity**. What is genuinely ours is not made genuine merely by reproducing it perfectly.

With these discussions establishing why machine experience, if it exists, may be different from human experience, we can also examine many arguments surrounding an AI doomsday scenario.

Can a superintelligent machine harm us, not through human misuse of it, which is a different matter, but through its own intentions?

I believe this question is often based on an unjustified assumption. The idea that a machine must develop harmful intentions of its own involves projecting human subjective experience onto a machine. This may work in science fiction, and it can certainly be entertaining, but it does not necessarily have much intellectual merit.

For a simple reason: as we have established earlier, even if machines have subjective experience, we have no reason to assume that it is the same as human experience. The concept of harmful intent, as we commonly understand it, is deeply rooted in human psychology.

Let us consider something closer to us: plants. Do plants have the intention to harm humans? Of course, we have no good reason to think so. Yet weeds can grow, destroy crops, and adversely affect human lives. Their natural life cycles can be harmful to us, but that is not the same as having an intention to harm us.

Machines are very different from plants, of course, but the distinction remains relevant. Something can cause harm without intending to do so.

Similarly, the idea that one species will evolve beyond another and destroy the weaker species assumes a particular kind of evolutionary drive. Biological evolution does not require an individual organism to consciously intend to evolve, but the analogy is often used to suggest that machines will inevitably develop an equivalent drive to survive, improve, and dominate. There is no reason to assume that this must happen.

The concept of biological evolution applies to living organisms. A calculator does not evolve on its own simply because it can perform calculations. Machines can certainly be modified, improved, and even help develop successor systems, but that does not establish that they possess an inherent evolutionary intent like the drives associated with biological organisms.

Just as weeds do not need an intention to harm us to cause harm, AI systems could cause harm if we do not handle them carefully.

In fact, I believe the more immediate danger is that AI could gradually diminish human creativity, critical thinking, and intuition.

How? Now that we have machines capable of mimicking our thinking and creativity, we may find that, for most of our purposes, their outputs are good enough. We may gradually lose the skills and habits associated with independent thinking and creativity.

We may eventually reach a point where we no longer know what is genuinely creative or intellectually insightful. Intelligence becomes abstracted from the individual, and we become increasingly dependent on machines to perform tasks that we once did ourselves.

This is dangerous because we risk evolving backwards, at least in an intellectual sense.

And here is the main catch: machines did not develop their capabilities independently of humanity. They were built using human knowledge, research, and work. Although they can generate new discoveries and contribute to further advances, their continued development depends on many factors. We also have no compelling reason to assume that they possess an inherent desire to evolve.

If we lose our own intellectual capabilities while becoming increasingly dependent on machines, we may reach a point where we can no longer independently understand, evaluate, or improve the systems we rely on. By then, considerable damage may already have been done.

I am not arguing here for accelerating or slowing down AI development. My point is simply that we should not regress intellectually as AI becomes more capable.

Although much of this discussion has been abstract, this particular concern is grounded in reality. The intellectual capabilities of AI have already become daunting to many people.

When OpenAI announced that AI agents had solved a Millennium Prize Problem, it would have been demoralising for many young, aspiring mathematicians. Personally, developments of this kind have been very demotivating for me, too.

This was one of the things that inspired me to write this essay, to clarify my thoughts and address this conflict. And, of course, I cannot help wondering: could a machine ever be genuinely inspired to write an essay like this?

I call this the *fear of AI*. We should not give in to it, because there is no reason to do so.

Our intellectual and creative endeavours have value. Our curiosity, critical thinking, songs, music, and art have value, regardless of machines' ability to mimic or even surpass us in producing their observable results. The fact that a machine can perform a task better than we can does not mean that our efforts to perform that task have become meaningless.

We should not allow these developments to take away our inspiration or discourage us from pursuing our intellectual and creative interests. If we do, the consequences could be truly catastrophic.

And with all this, I say:

**There is no reason to fear machines, but every reason to fear the fear of machines.**
